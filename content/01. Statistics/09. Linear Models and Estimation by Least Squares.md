## Introduction
- In Chapter [[07. Properties of Point Estimators and Methods of Estimation]], we considered several methods for finding estimators of parameters, including the methods of moments and maximum likelihood and also methods based on sufficient statistics. Another method of estimation, the method of least squares, is the topic of this chapter.
- 이전 장에서는 Method of Moment, Maximum Likelihood Estimation을 통해서 점 추정을 하는 방법에 대해서 배웠으며 이번 장에서는 Method of Least Squares를 통해서 추정하는 방법을 알아볼 예정이다. 
- In all our previous discussions of statistical inference, we assumed that the observable random variable $Y_{1}, Y_{2}, \dots, Y_{n}$ were independent and identical distributed. 
	- One implication of this assumption is that the expected value of $Y_i, E(Y_{i})$, is constant.
	- 만약 값이 존재한다면.
- In this chapter, we undertake a study of inferential procedures that can be used when a random variable $Y$, called the $dependent\, variable$, has a mean that is a function of one or more nonrandom variables $x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{k}$, called $independent \, variables$.
	- Y : 종속변수, 즉 어떤 값들에 의해 정해지는 변수
	- $x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{k}$ : 독립변수, 즉 다른 변수에 영향을 받지 않는 변수
	- in this context, the terms independent and dependent are used in their mathematical sense. There is no relationship with the probabilistic concept of independent random variables.

**Deterministic mathematical model**
$$
y = \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x
$$
- where $\beta_{0}\ \text{and} \, \beta_{1}$ are known parameters. 
- it does not allow for any error in predicting $y$ as a function of $x$.
- 즉, 우리는 에러 텀을 인정하지 않고 $\beta$만 정해진다면 y의 값이 결정되는 것을 Deterministic mathematical model이라 부른다.

## Linear Statistical Models
- We will concentrate on a set of models called ==linear statistical models==. If $Y$ is the response variable and $x$ is a single independent variable, it may be reasonable in some situations to use the model $E(Y) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x$ for unknown parameter values $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$. Notice that in this model $E(Y)$ is a linear function of $x$ (for a given $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$) and also a linear function of $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$ \[because $E(Y) = c\beta_{0} + d\beta_{1}$ with $c=1$ and $d=x$].
- In the model $E(Y) = \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x^2$ is not a linear function of $x$, but it is a linear function of $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$ \[because $E(Y) = c\beta_{0} + d\beta_{1}$ with $c=1$ and $d=x^2$].
- When we say we have a linear statistical model for $Y$, we mean that $E(Y)$ is a linear function of the unknown parameters $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$ and not necessarily a linear function of $x$. Thus, $Y= \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\ln x+\epsilon$ is a linear model.
- 따라서 우리가 지칭하는 Linear model은 $Y$에 대한 평균값이 $\beta$에 대해서 선형인 식을 의미한다. 

**DEFINITION 11.1**
- A $linear \, statistical$ model relating a random response $Y$ to a set of independent variables $x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{k}$ is of the form

$$
Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1}+ \beta_{2}x_{2}+\cdots + \beta_{k}x_{k} + \epsilon,
$$
- where $\beta_{0}, \beta_{1}, \dots, \beta_{k}$ are unknown parameters, $\epsilon$ is a random variable, and the variables $x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{k}$ assume known values. We will assume that $E(\epsilon) = 0$ and hence that
$$
E(Y) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1}+ \beta_{2}x_{2}+\cdots + \beta_{k}x_{k}.
$$

## The Method of Least Squares
- Suppose that we wish to fit the model
$$
E(Y) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x
$$
- That is, we postulate that $Y= \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ possesses som probability distribution with $E(\epsilon) = 0$. 
- If $\hat{\beta_{0}}$ and $\hat{\beta_{1}}$ are estimators of the parameters $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$, then $\hat{Y} = \hat{\beta_{0}} + \hat{\beta_{1}}x$ is clearly an estimator $E(Y)$
$$
\hat{y}_{i} = \hat{\beta}_{0} + \hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i}
$$
- is the predicted values of the $i$th $y$ values (when $x=x_{i}$), then the deviation (sometimes called the error) of the observed value of $y_{i}$ from $\hat{y}_{i} = \hat{\beta}_{0} + \hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i}$ is the difference $y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i}$ and  the sum of squares of deviations to be minimized is
$$
\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^n(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n[y_{i} - (\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})]^2
$$
- ![[Pasted image 20250104154032.png]]
- The quantity SSE is also called the **sum of squares for error** for reasons that will subsequently become apparent.
- If SSE possesses a minimum, it will occur for values of $\beta_{0}$ and $\beta_{1}$ that satisfy the equations, $\partial SSE/\partial \hat{\beta}_{0}=0$ and $\partial SSE/\partial\hat{\beta}_{1}=0$.
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial \hat{\beta}_{0}} & = \frac{\partial\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n[y_{i} - (\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})]^2 \right\}}{\partial \hat{\beta}_{0}} \\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^n 2[y_{i}-(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})] \\
&= -2\left(\sum_{i=1}^ny_{i} - n\hat{\beta}_{0} - \hat{\beta}_{1}\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i} \right) = 0. \\
 \\
\text{and} \\
\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial \hat{\beta}_{1}} & = \frac{\partial\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n[y_{i}- (\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})]^2 \right\}}{} \\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^n 2[y_{i}-(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})]x_{i} \\
&= -2\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i}y_{i} - \hat{\beta}_{0}\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i} -\hat{\beta}_{1}\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i}^2\right) = 0.
\end{align}
$$
- The equations $\partial SSE/\hat{\beta}_{0}=0$ and $\partial SSE/\hat{\beta}_{1}=0$ are called the **least-squares equations** for estimating the parameters of a line. 
$$
\begin{align} 
& \hat{\beta}_{1} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i} -\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i}-\bar{x})^2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i}y_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i}\sum_{i=1}^ny_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i}^2 - \frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i}\right)^2}, \\
& \hat{\beta}_{0}=\bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_{1}\bar{x}.
\end{align}
$$

**Least-Squares Estimators for the Simple Linear Regression Model**
1. $\hat{\beta}_{1} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}$, where $S_{xy} = \sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}- \bar{y})$ and $S_{xx} = \sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2$
2. $\hat{\beta}_{0}= \bar{y}-\hat{\beta}_{1}\bar{x}$

## Properties of the Least-Squares Estimators: Simple Linear Regression
- We need to determine the statistical properties of least-squares estimators if we wish to use them ==to make statistical inferences==. In this section, we show that the least-squares estimators $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ for the parameters in the simple linear model are ==unbiased estimators== of their respective parameters values.
$$
Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x + \epsilon
$$
- Recall that $\epsilon$ was previously assumed to be a random variable with $E(\epsilon) = 0$. We now add the assumption that $V(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$. That is, we are assuming that the difference between the random variable $Y$ and $E(Y) = \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x$ is distributed about zero with a variance that does not depend on $x$. Notice that $V(Y) = V(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$
- Assume that $n$ independent observations are to be made on this model so that before sampling we have $n$ independent random variables of the form.
$$
Y_{I}= \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i} + \epsilon_{i}
$$
- From Section 9.3, we know that
$$
\begin{align}
\hat{\beta}_{1} &= \frac{S_{x y}}{S_{x x}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})(Y_{i}-\bar{Y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i}-\bar{x})^2} \\
&= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i}-\bar{x})Y_{i}- \bar{Y}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})}{S_{x x}} \\
&= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})Y_{i}}{S_{x x}}
\end{align}
$$
- Because $\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})=0$.
$$
\begin{align}
& \sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^nx_{i } - n\bar{x}. \\ \\

& n\bar{x} = n\times \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nx_{i} \\
 \\
& \therefore \sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x}) =0
\end{align}
$$

- From the expectation theorems developed in Section 5.8, we have
$$
\begin{align}
E(\hat{\beta}_{1}) &= E\left[\frac{\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})Y_{i}}{S_{x x}}\right] = \frac{\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})E(Y_{i})}{S_{x x}} \\
& = \frac{\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i})}{S_{x x}} \\
& = \beta_{0}\frac{\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})}{S_{x x}} + \beta_{1}\frac{\sum(x_{i} - \bar{x})x_{i}}{S_{x x}} \\
& = 0 + \beta_{1}\frac{S_{x x}}{S_{x x}} = \beta_{1}
\end{align}
$$
- Because $\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})=0$ and $S_{x x} = \sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2 = \sum (x_{i} - \bar{x})x_{i}$
$$
\begin{align}
S_{x x} &= \sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2 \\
& = \sum(x_{i}^2-2x_{i}\bar{x} +\bar{x}^2) \\
&= \sum x_{i}^2 -2 \bar{x}\sum x_{i} + n \bar{x}^2 \\
& = \sum x_{i}^2 +\bar{x} \left( -2\sum x_{i} +n\bar{x} \right) \\
& = \sum x_{i}^2 +\bar{x}\left( -2\sum x_{i} + \sum x_{i} \right) \\
& = \sum (x_{i} - \bar{x})x_{i}
\end{align}
$$
- Thus, $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ is unbiased estimator of $\beta_{1}$
- To find $V(\hat{\beta}_{1})$, we use Theorem 5.12. Recall that $Y_{1}, Y_{2}, \dots, Y_{n}$ are independent and, therefore,
$$
\begin{align}
V(\hat{\beta}_{1}) & = V\left[\frac{\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})Y_{i}}{S_{x x}}\right] = \left[\frac{1}{S_{xx}}\right]^2\sum V[(x_{i}-\bar{x})Y_{i}] \\
& = \left[\frac{1}{S_{xx}}\right]^2 \sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2V(Y_{i}) \\
& = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{x x}}
\end{align}
$$
- Because $V(Y_{i}) = \sigma^2$, for $i=1, 2, \dots, n$. and $(x_{i}-\bar{x})$ is constant.
- Now let us find the expected value and variance of $\hat{\beta}_{0}$, where $\hat{\beta}_{0} = \bar{Y} -\hat{\beta}_{1}\bar{x}$. from Theorem 5.12, we have
$$
V(\hat{\beta}_{0}) = V(\bar{Y}) + \bar{x}^2V(\hat{\beta}_{1}) -2\bar{x}Cov(\bar{Y}, \hat{\beta}_{1})
$$
- Consequently, we must find $V(\bar{Y})$ and $Cov(\bar{Y}, \hat{\beta}_{1})$ in order to obtain $V(\hat{\beta}_{0})$. Because $Y_{i}= \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i}+\epsilon_{i}$.

$$
\begin{align}
&\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum Y_{i} = \beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\bar{x} + \bar{\epsilon}. \\
\text{Thus,} \\
 & E(\bar{Y}) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}\bar{x}+E(\bar{\epsilon}) = \beta_{0}+ \beta_{1}\bar{x} \\
\text{and} \\
 & V(\bar{Y}) = V(\bar{\epsilon}) = \frac{1}{n}V(\epsilon_{1}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
\end{align}
$$
- To find $Cov(\bar{Y}, \hat{\beta}_{1})$, rewrite the expression for $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ as
$$
\begin{align}
 & \hat{\beta}_{1} = \sum c_{i}Y_{i}, \\
\text{Where} \\
 & c_{i}= \frac{x_{i}-\bar{x}}{S_{x x}} \\
&\text{(Notice that)} \sum c_i=0 \,\text{Then}, \\
 Cov(\bar{Y}, \hat{\beta}_{1}) &= Cov\left[\sum\frac{1}{n}Y_{i}, \sum c_{i}Y_{i}\right], \\ 
& = \sum\left( \frac{c_{i}}{n}V(Y_{i}) \right) + {\sum \sum}_{i \neq j}\left( \frac{c_{j}}{n}\right) Cov(Y_{i}, Y_{j}) \\
 & =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum c_{i} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum\left(\frac{x_{i}-\bar{x}}{S_{x x}}\right) = 0
\end{align}
$$
- Because $Y_{i}$ and $Y_{j}$, where $i\neq j$, are independent, $Cov(Y_{i}, Y_{j}) = 0$. Also, $V(Y_{i}) = \sigma^2$
- Returning to our original task of finding the expected value and variance of $\hat{\beta}_{0} = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_{1}\bar{x}$, we apply expectation theorems to obtain
$$
E(\hat{\beta}_{0}) = E(\bar{Y}) - E(\hat{\beta}_{1})\bar{x} = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}\bar{x} - \beta_{1}\bar{x} = \beta_{0}
$$
- Thus, we have shown that both $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ are unbiased estimators of their respective parameters.
$$
\begin{align}
V(\hat{\beta}_{0}) & = V(\bar{Y}) + \bar{x}^2V(\hat{\beta}_{1}) - 2\bar{x}Cov(\bar{Y}, \hat{\beta}_{1}) \\
 & = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} +\bar{x}^2\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{x x}} - 0 \\
 & = \sigma^2\left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2 }{S_{x x}}\right) \\
 &  = \frac{\sigma^2\sum x_{i}^2}{nS_{x x}}
\end{align}
$$
- Further, Theorem 5.12 can be employed to show that Notice that $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ are correlated (and therefore dependent) unless $\bar{x} = 0$.
$$
Cov(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{1}) = \frac{-\bar{x}\sigma^2}{S_{x x}}
$$

- The preceding expression give the variances for the least-squares estimators in terms of $\sigma^2$, the variance of the error term $\epsilon$. Usually the value of $\sigma^2$ is unknown, and we will need to make use of the sample observations to estimate $\sigma^2$. If $\bar{Y}$ is used to estimate the mean, we previously used to estimate the population variance $\sigma^2$. Because we are using $\bar{Y}$ to estimate $E(Y_{i})$, it seems natural to base an estimate of $\sigma^2$ on $SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i} - \hat{Y}_{i})^2$. 
$$
\left( \frac{1}{n-1} \right)\sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_{i}-\bar{Y})^2
$$
- Indeed, we will show that provides an unbiased estimator for $\sigma^2$.
$$
S^2 = \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)\sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_{i}-\hat{Y}_{i})^2 = \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)SSE
$$
- Notice that the 2 occurring in the denominator of $S^2$ corresponds to the number of $\beta$ parameters estimated in the model. it necessary to find E(SSE) in order to verify that $E(S^2) = \sigma^2$.
$$
\begin{align}
\text{Because} \\
&E(S^2) = E\left[ \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)SSE \right] = \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)E(SSE)
\end{align}
$$
- Notice that
$$
\begin{align}
E(SSE) & = E\left[ \sum(Y_{i}-\hat{Y}_{i})^2 \right] = E\left[ \sum(Y_{i}- \hat{\beta}_{0} - \hat{\beta}_{1} x_{i})^2 \right] \\
 &  = E\left[ \sum (Y_{i}- \bar{Y}+ \hat{\beta}_{1}\bar{x} - \hat{\beta}_{1}x_{i})^2  \right] \\
 &  = E\left[ \sum[(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}) - \hat{\beta}_{1}(x_{i}-\bar{x})]^2 \right] \\
 &  = E\left[ \sum(Y_{i}-\bar{Y})^2 + \hat{\beta}_{1}^2\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2 -2\hat{\beta}_{1}\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}) \right] \\
\end{align}
$$
- $\hat{\beta}_{1} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{x x}} \to S_{xy} = S_{xx}\hat{\beta}_{1}$, the last two terms in the expectation combine to give $-\hat{\beta}_{1}\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})^2$. Also,  $\sum(Y_{i}-\bar{Y})^2 = \sum Y_{i}^2 - n\bar{Y}^2$ and, therefore,
$$
\begin{align}
E\left[ \sum(Y_{i}- \hat{Y})^2 \right] &= E\left[ \sum Y_{i}^2 -n\bar{Y}^2 - \hat{\beta}_{1}^2S_{xx} \right] \\
 & = \sum E(Y_{i}^2) - nE(\bar{Y}^2) - S_{xx}E(\hat{\beta}_{1}^2)
\end{align}
$$
- Noting that, for any random variable $U$, $E(U^2) = V(U) + [E(U)]^2$, we see that 
$$
\begin{align}
E\left[ \sum(Y_{i}-\hat{Y}_{i})^2 \right] & = \sum\{V(Y_{i}) + [E(Y_{i})]^2\} - n\{V(\bar{Y})+[E(\bar{V})]^2\} - S_{xx}\{V(\hat{\beta}_{1})+[E(\hat{\beta}_{1})]^2\} \\
 &  = n\sigma^2+\sum(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i})^2 -n\left[ \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + (\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\bar{x})^2 \right] - S_{xx}\left( \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \beta_{1}^2\right)
\end{align}
$$
- This expression simplifies to $(n-2)\sigma^2$. Thus, we find that an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$ is given by
$$
\begin{align} \\
S^2 &= \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)\sum(Y_{i}-\hat{Y}_{i})^2 = \left( \frac{1}{n-2} \right)SSE  \\
SSE &=  \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \bar{y})^2 - \hat{\beta}_{1}\sum(x_{i}-\bar{x})(y_{i}-\bar{y}) \\
 & =S_{yy}-\hat{\beta}_{1}S_{xy}, \, \text{where} \, S_{yy} = \sum(y_{i}-\bar{y})^2
\end{align}
$$
- $S^2 = SSE/(n-2)$ is an unbiased estimator for the parameter $\sigma^2$. Thus far, the only assumptions that we have made about the error term $\epsilon$ in the model $Y=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x + \epsilon$ is that $E(\epsilon) = 0$ and $V(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$, independent of $x$. 
- The form of the sampling distributions for $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ depends on the distribution of the error term $\epsilon$. Because of the common occurrence of the normal distribution in nature, it is often reasonable to assume that $\epsilon$ is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$.
- If this assumption of normality is warranted, it follows that $Y_{i}$ is normally distributed with mean $\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Further, if the assumption of normality is warranted, it follows that
$$
\frac{(n-2)S^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{SSE}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(dof=(n-2))
$$
- As you will subsequently see, the assumption of normality of the distribution of the error term $\epsilon$ and the resulting normal distributions for $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ will allow us to develop tests and confidence intervals based on the $t$ distribution.

**Properties of the Least-Squares Estimators; Simple Linear Regression**
1. The estimators $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ are unbiased - that is, $E(\hat{\beta}_{i}) = \beta_{i}$, for $i=0, 1$
2. $V(\hat{\beta}_{0})= c_{00}\sigma^2$, where $c_{00}=\sum x_{i}/nS_{xx}$
3. $V(\hat{\beta}_{1}) = c_{11}\sigma^2$, where $c_{11}=1/S_{xx}$
4. $Cov(\hat{\beta}_{0}, \hat{\beta}_{1}) = c_{01}\sigma^2$, where $c_{01}= -\bar{x}/S_{xx}$
5. An unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$ is $S^2 = SSE/(n-2)$, where $SSE=S_{yy}-\hat{\beta}S_{xy}$ and $S_{yy}=\sum(y_{i}-\bar{y})^2$
If, in addition, the $\epsilon_{i}$, for $i=1, 2, \dots, n$ are normally distributed, 
6. Both $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ are normally distributed.
7. The random variable $(n-2)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(dof=(n-2))$
8. The statistic $S^2$ is independent of both $\hat{\beta}_{0}$ and $\hat{\beta}_{1}$

## Inferences Concerning the Parameters $\beta_{i}$
- Suppose that an engineer has fit the model
$$
Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x + \epsilon,
$$
- $H_{0} : \beta_{1} =0$ $\to$ Does Not Exist Effect
- $H_{a}: \beta_{1}\neq 0 \to$ Exist Effect
	- The independent variable has an influence on the dependent variable.
- In general, for any linear regression model, if the random error $\epsilon$ is normally distributed, we have established that $\hat{\beta}_{1}$ is an unbiased, normally distributed estimator of $\beta_{i}$ with
$$
\begin{align}
 & V(\hat{\beta}_{0}) = c_{00}\sigma^2, \qquad \text{where} \, c_{00} = \frac{\sum x_{i}^2}{nS_{xx}} \\
\text{and} \\
 & V(\hat{\beta}_{1} )= c_{11}\sigma^2, \qquad \text{where} \, c_{11} = \frac{1}{S_{xx}}
\end{align}
$$
- That is, the variances of both estimators are constant multiples of $\sigma^2$, the variance of the error term in the model. Using this information, we can construct a test of the hypothesis $H_{0}:\beta_{i}=\beta_{i0}$ ($\beta_{i 0}$ is specified value of $\beta_{i}$), using the test statistic
$$
\begin{align}
 & Z = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{i} - \beta_{0}}{\sigma \sqrt{ c_{ii} }} \\
\text{where} \\
 & c_{00} = \frac{\sum x_{i}^2}{nS_{xx}} \quad \text{and} \quad c_{11}=\frac{1}{S_{xx}}
\end{align}
$$
- The rejection region for a two-tailed test is given by $|z| \geq z_{\alpha/2}$. As in the case of the simple $Z$ tests studied in Chapter 10, to compute either of the preceding $Z$ statistics, we must either know $\sigma$ or possess a good estimate based on adequate number of degrees of freedom.
- So, We don't know $\sigma$ (Variance of Population). So we need to estimate $\sigma$. if we estimate $\sigma$ with $S = \sqrt{ SSE / (n-2) }$, the resulting quantity can be shown to possess a Student's $t$ distribution with $n-2$ df.
$$
T = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{i} - \beta_{i 0}}{S\sqrt{ c_{ii} }}
$$

###### Test of Hypothesis for $\beta_{i}$
$$
\begin{align}
 & H_{0} : \beta_{i} = \beta_{i 0}. \\
 & H_{a} :  
\begin{cases}
\beta_{i } > \beta_{i 0} \qquad (\text{upper-tail rejection region}),  \\
\beta_{i} < \beta_{i 0} \qquad (\text{lower-tail rejection region}),  \\
\beta_{i} \neq \beta_{i 0} \qquad (\text{two-tail rejection region}).
\end{cases} \\
 &  \text{Test statistic}: \, T = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{i} - \beta_{i 0}}{S\sqrt{ c_{ii} }} \\
 & \text{Rejection region}:  
\begin{cases}
\, t>t_{\alpha} \qquad  &(\text{upper-tail alternative}), \\
\, t< -t_{\alpha} \qquad &(\text{lower-tail alternative}), \\
\, |t| > t_{\alpha/2} \qquad  & (\text{two-tailed alternative}),
\end{cases} \\
 & \text{where}  \qquad  c_{00} = \frac{\sum x_{i}^2}{nS_{xx}} \quad \text{and} \quad c_{11} = \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \\
 & \text{Notice that} \, t_{\alpha} \, \text{is based on (n-2) df.}
\end{align}
$$

###### A $(100)(1-\alpha)$% Confidence Interval for $\beta_{i}$
$$
\begin{align}
 & \hat{\beta}_{i} \pm t_{\alpha/2}S\sqrt{ c_{ii} }, \\
\text{where} \\
 & c_{00} =\frac{\sum x_{i}^2}{nS_{xx}} \quad \text{and} \quad c_{11}=\frac{1}{S_{xx}}
